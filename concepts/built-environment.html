When Light Meets Metabolism

Shade, Glass, and Indoors

His meter reads almost no infrared behind low-E glass, and a full dose in the shade.

Outside: sunlight, and infrared reflected from greenery. Inside: LED bulbs and low-E glass. He says the built environment is not good for us.

He measured his own dining room with a Circadian Innovations sensor he calls the My Eye. He had replaced the windows years earlier, at high cost, with low-E glass, before he knew this literature. Side windows open, one wall of glass, and the sensor still read 0.810 watts per square meter.

"the amount of infrared light that's coming in to my sensor is 0.810 W per square meter."

He says you need about 29 watts per square meter to match a study in which COVID patients got out of hospital faster. In the front yard, in the shade, under trees, with sun on the surrounding greenery, the sensor was over 29. He then tells people who burn not to stand in the sun. Cover up, wear a broad-brimmed hat and long sleeves, or sit in the shade under a tree. The figure he attaches there is 41.9 watts per square meter.

Keep the three readings separate. 0.810 indoors. Over 29 under the trees. 41.9 in the shade under a tree. He does not average them.

"you don't need to go in the sun. You can cover up."

Indoors wins the day, he says, because it is comfortable and it holds the screens. What he thinks people ought to do is go into the outdoor light.

High latitude

A questioner asks about dark northern winters and indoor bulbs. Diseases sort by latitude, he says. Before effective blood-pressure drugs, population blood pressure rose as you moved from the equator. Ultraviolet makes nitric oxide in the skin, a vasodilator. Multiple sclerosis and other diseases sort by latitude too. Those are not the same mechanism as the infrared story.

He used to live in Toronto. His picture of winter sun is black ice: the day after a snow, the sun can melt it even at high latitude. At his house, about a mile up in Southern California, roof drips ran while the sun hit the snow and stopped at sunset, though the air temperature had not changed. He calls that infrared heating the ice locally.

"the problem is not with the sun at those latitudes. The problem is with our social construct"

The failure mode is the schedule. Up late, inside all day, home after dark, possibly weeks without outdoor sun. His plan is to get outside during the short daylight, for example at lunch. Solariums help when the glass lets the sun through and does not block it. That is a different glass from the low-E panes in the dining room. He says there are solutions short of moving.

He does not give a lamp specification in answer to the bulb question. The answer he gives is outdoor time, and glass that is not blocking.
