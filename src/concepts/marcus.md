# Marcus Theory

**Lambda is reorganization energy, not wavelength. Where the curves cross decides whether infrared speeds the step or slows it.**

Delta G, the Gibbs free energy, is what he says you can look up for each redox step. It is the energy available to pump protons. It is measured as if the reaction sat in a test tube. In the protein, something else has to happen before the electron moves. Side chains twist. The solvent shell reorganizes: the water around the donor, which is about to become positive, turns its negative faces inward, and the shell around the acceptor turns the other way. Then the electron moves without a further energy cost.

On the diagram, the reactant sits at the bottom of a parabola. Twisting climbs the curve. The height of that climb is lambda.

> "please don't confuse this lambda for wavelength. This lambda is not wavelength. This lambda actually is the reorganization energy."

Infrared, in this account, supplies the vibrational modes that pay that cost. Delta G and lambda stay the same. What changes is how often the molecules are up near the crossing.

## Three cases

- **A barrier.** The crossing is up the reactant curve. Vibration pushes molecules up to it. The step speeds up.
- **Activationless.** The crossing is already at the bottom. The step goes without help. Infrared pushes molecules off that point, and the step slows down.
- **The inverted region.** Drop delta G further and the product arm climbs the far side. The crossing is high again. You have to put energy back in. Marcus predicted this in the 1980s. He says another group built a molecule in 1988 and saw the inverted curve. He shows Marcus receiving the prize from the king of Sweden in 1992.

> "It would push it further up the parabola where there is no crossing point, which means that reaction would actually slow down."

Applied to every cell: a low crossing plus infrared slows that step, a high crossing plus infrared speeds it. Different steps along the chain can sit in different places, so one exposure can do both.

## Shown in a chemical, not in a mitochondrion

The experiment he walks through next is a constructed molecule. A UV pulse creates the electron on the donor. Mid-infrared vibrates a bridge. The electron moves to an N3 acceptor, then returns. The forward crossing is at the bottom, so infrared slows charge separation. The return crossing is higher, so infrared speeds charge recombination. He reads the paper in those terms. The caption writes "IR exhortation" where the paper is talking about excitation. The authors, as he reads them, locate the change in the density of states at the curve crossing.

> "The forward ET reaction is near activationless."

He is explicit: this is what they propose for mitochondria, demonstrated in a model that is not a mitochondrion. A further citation he speaks as "Delore at all" says that selectively exciting a vibration with infrared can alter electron-transfer yields and even switch one charge-separation path off.

A separate result: structured water placed between two charges increased the transfer rate a thousandfold. He then says structured water can change mitochondrial electron transfer by over a thousandfold. He is explicit that this experiment used no infrared. He puts it on the coupling factor in Marcus's equation, next to lambda.

Nobody, he says later, has isolated each mitochondrial transfer, measured the reorganization energies, and swept wavelength. The caption says "organization energies" at that moment. There are more reactions than the four complexes. An activationless step slows if you vibrate it. A high barrier speeds up. See [Microbes and Wavelength Maps](microbes.md).
