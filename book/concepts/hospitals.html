When Light Meets Metabolism

Hospitals and Heliotherapy

He wants the drugs and the sunlight. His history is why the buildings dropped the sunlight.

What he shows now

Sheets of light on ICU patients. He says the diagnosis did not matter. Then three buildings:

  • Footscray, Australia, a few miles west of Melbourne. A 1.5 billion dollar hospital whose ICU is built so patients can go out for sun and fresh air.
  • King's College Hospital, London. Two million pounds on the rooftop, because that was where downtown could expand. A 60-bed ICU downstairs. Patients rotate up for air, sunlight, and plants. The first patient he names went up on 28 May 2026, after weeks downstairs.
  • St George, London. The same idea for the neuro ICU. Queen Camilla visited.

The CEO at King's, he says, hopes for shorter stays. Seheult then applies the 30 percent figure from the light studies himself. In a single-payer system, a 30 percent shorter stay would cut pharmacy and nursing about 30 percent, and "this actually pencils." That arithmetic is his "if." He does not report a measured 30 percent from Footscray, King's, or St George.

Infrared, he says, still enters through the blanket and the gown.

Why he thinks the older practice stopped

The question is about heliotherapy in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and why the research seemed to drop. The caption breaks the question across "Dr." and "lupus." No name is filled in here.

He names Rollier and John Harvey Kellogg. In the early 1900s the Battle Creek sanitarium, run by Kellogg, was the largest hospital in the world. He says the town had about 14 cereal companies and that Kellogg's brother founded one. Kellogg invented light boxes and learned from European high-altitude tuberculosis sanitariums. The building was part of treatment.

What changed, in his account:

  • World War II, and the need to expand hospitals. Large windows, fresh air, and sunlight mattered less to architects.
  • Penicillin. Julius Wagner-Jauregg won the 1927 Nobel for showing that fever, including artificial fever, could kill neurosyphilis. Penicillin was discovered in 1928. Artificial heating stayed standard into the 1940s and 1950s, then lost because it was laborious.
  • A 1942 architectural article he says he has. Hallways on the outside, storage in the core, so some rooms had no direct sun and no outside air. Sold as cheap expansion and more fee-for-service beds.
  • Randomized placebo-controlled trials became the gate for a new therapy.

Florence Nightingale's observation, from the late 1850s through the 1900s, was that fresh air and sunlight were best for her patients. Then the switch, toward medications and toward hospitals as housing for interventions.

"I'm not against the use of technology and medications."

He wants both. London and Australia are the example: high technology inside, patients outside for sun and air. The host closes by hoping architects design for light.
